# The Pop-Bang effects

Ten effects ship in [`tools/edzco-sound/sounds/`](../tools/edzco-sound/sounds/), generated by [`make_sounds.py`](make_sounds.py). They are synthesised, not recorded. This is why, and how to use your own instead.

## Why not real recordings

Two reasons, and the second is the one that actually decided it.

### Licensing

A recording of an exhaust is someone's car and someone's licence. The permissively-licensed material that exists is thin:

| Source | Licence | What is actually there |
|---|---|---|
| [Freesound — CeebFrack `BACKFIRE.ogg`](https://freesound.org/people/CeebFrack/sounds/105351/) | CC0 | One 2 s backfire, assembled from other Freesound samples |
| [Freesound — nsstudios `backfiring vehicle`](https://freesound.org/people/nsstudios/sounds/351738/) | CC0 | 3.6 s, synthetic, a vehicle slowing with one backfire |
| [buntine/CarEngines](https://github.com/buntine/CarEngines) | CC0 | Procedural WebAudio, no samples |
| [Kidrage/VehicleSoundSynth](https://github.com/Kidrage/VehicleSoundSynth) | — | Max 8 patches, synthesises backfires, no files |
| [pmndrs/racing-game](https://github.com/pmndrs/racing-game) | CC0 assets | No exhaust audio found |
| [code4fukui/sound-cc0](https://github.com/code4fukui/sound-cc0) | CC0 | Bells, crackers, switches — no car sounds |

Most "free backfire sound" sites are AI generators or meme soundboards with unclear terms, which is not something to bundle into a repo other people install from.

### The bigger problem: those clips are the wrong shape

Every CC0 backfire found is **one bang** — a single report, the sort of thing a film uses for a gunshot stand-in. A real overrun is not that.

On a lift-off the throttle shuts, the engine pumps air, and fuel that did not burn in the cylinder lights in the hot exhaust. So:

- it is never one pop — it is a **run** of them, as each cylinder's charge goes off;
- the spacing is **irregular**. Evenly spaced pops read as a machine gun, not a car;
- it **decays**: the revs are falling, so the pops thin out and quieten;
- each pop is a transient (the crack) + the pipe ringing (the body) + a pressure thump;
- the pipe resonance barely moves between pops — it is the same pipe — but the crack's brightness varies a lot with how much fuel went off.

Dropping a single-bang sample on a lift would sound like a backfire effect. The synthesis models the list above, so it sounds like an overrun.

## The ten

| id | Level | Character |
|---|---|---|
| `tick` | 1 | Two soft ticks. A gentle lift at low revs. |
| `pop` | 1 | One clean report. |
| `double` | 2 | Two, the second deeper — how a real pair lands. |
| `crackle_light` | 2 | A short stutter, bright and thin. |
| `crackle_med` | 3 | The everyday overrun: a dozen irregular cracks fading out. |
| `burble_slow` | 2 | Low and round, loafing. Less crack, more chest. |
| `burble_fast` | 3 | The same depth, closer together. |
| `crackle_heavy` | 4 | Long and busy, the way a tuned car carries on after the lift. |
| `bang` | 4 | One big one. Deep, slow to let go. |
| `bang_tail` | 5 | A big one, then the small ones falling away behind it. |

`level` is matched against how hard the lift was — see the [app README](../apps/edzco_popbang/README.md).

## Tuning them

Everything is parameters in [`make_sounds.py`](make_sounds.py). The shared `burst()` builds a run of pops:

```python
burst(rng, count,
      pipe_f=480,            # the pipe's resonance - lower is a deeper bang
      pipe_q=3.6,            # how rung-out it is
      gap_ms=(36, 104),      # spacing; the irregularity is what sells it
      decay_ms=(30, 68),     # how long each report lasts
      bright=(0.6, 1.0),     # how much crack rides over the resonance
      fade=0.91,             # gain per pop, so the run decays like falling revs
      jitter=0.85)           # how far the gaps wander
```

Change a number, run `python docs/make_sounds.py`, and all ten rebuild in about a second. Seeds are fixed per effect, so a rebuild is byte-identical and the repo does not churn.

## Using your own recordings

The tool does not care where the files came from:

1. Put 48 kHz stereo WAVs in `tools/edzco-sound/sounds/`.
2. Either add them to `sounds.json` (`id`, `name`, `level` 1–5, `file`, `durationMs`), or delete `sounds.json` — without it the tool falls back to listing every `.wav` in the folder.
3. Restart the service.

48 kHz stereo matches the device natively. Other rates still work — the device is opened through ALSA's `plug:`, which converts — but converting on the head unit is work it does not need to do.

If you record your own car: a phone behind the exhaust on a lift from about 4000 rpm, somewhere legal and empty, gives you far better material than anything in the table above, and it is unambiguously yours.
